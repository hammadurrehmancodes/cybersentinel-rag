# NIST SP 800-61 Rev 2 — Computer Security Incident Handling Guide

## Section: Incident Response Lifecycle

The incident response process has four phases: Preparation, Detection and Analysis, Containment Eradication and Recovery, and Post-Incident Activity. These phases are cyclical — lessons from post-incident reviews feed back into preparation for future incidents.

---

## Section: Preparation

Preparation is the most important phase. Organizations should establish an incident response capability before incidents occur.

Key preparation steps:
- Create an incident response policy that defines what constitutes an incident, establishes organizational structure, and defines roles and responsibilities
- Develop incident response procedures with step-by-step instructions for handling each incident category
- Build an incident response team with designated personnel, contact information, and escalation paths
- Acquire and maintain tools: forensic workstations, blank removable media, portable hard drives, network cables, laptops, evidence bags, chain of custody forms
- Establish communication guidelines — who gets notified, when, and how
- Conduct incident response training and exercises at least annually
- Create and maintain network diagrams, asset inventories, and baseline system profiles

---

## Section: Detection and Analysis — Identifying Incidents

Signs of incidents fall into two categories: precursors and indicators.

Precursors (signs an incident may occur in the future):
- Web server log entries showing vulnerability scanning
- Announcement of a new exploit targeting software in your environment
- Threat intelligence indicating your organization is a target

Indicators (signs an incident is occurring or has occurred):
- Antivirus alert detecting malware
- Multiple failed login attempts to an account
- Unexpected system reboots or crashes
- New administrator accounts created without authorization
- System logs showing access at unusual hours
- Network traffic to known malicious IP addresses
- Files modified or deleted without authorization

Incident Analysis steps:
1. Profile networks and systems to establish normal baseline behavior
2. Understand normal behavior before you can recognize abnormal
3. Create a log retention policy — logs older than 90 days often cannot support investigations
4. Correlate events across multiple sources (firewall, IDS, system logs)
5. Keep all clocks synchronized using NTP — timestamps that disagree destroy investigations
6. Maintain and use a knowledge base of known attack signatures
7. Use the Internet to research unusual activity before concluding it is malicious
8. Diagnose indicators with a structured process — ask what could cause this, not what do I think caused this

Incident prioritization factors:
- Functional impact: how much does this affect business operations right now
- Information impact: has confidential data been accessed or exfiltrated
- Recoverability: how long will recovery take and how much will it cost

## Section: Detection and Analysis — Brute Force Attacks

A brute force attack involves systematically attempting many passwords or passphrases to gain unauthorized access.

Indicators:
- Multiple failed authentication attempts from a single source IP
- Failed attempts targeting many different accounts from one IP (credential stuffing)
- Successful login immediately following many failures (likely successful breach)
- Authentication attempts at unusual hours or from unusual geographic locations

Severity escalation rules:
- More than 5 failed attempts in 1 minute: suspicious, begin monitoring
- More than 20 failed attempts in 5 minutes: likely brute force, alert and consider blocking
- More than 100 failed attempts in 1 hour: confirmed brute force, block and investigate
- Any successful login following a series of failures: treat as compromised account

Windows Event IDs to monitor:
- 4625: Failed logon
- 4648: Logon using explicit credentials
- 4740: Account lockout triggered
- 4624: Successful logon (check after a series of 4625 events)

Linux/SSH log indicators:
- "Failed password for [user] from [IP]" repeated entries in /var/log/auth.log
- "Invalid user [user] from [IP]"
- "error: maximum authentication attempts exceeded"

Immediate response actions:
1. Block the source IP at the perimeter firewall immediately
2. Lock the targeted accounts temporarily
3. Notify account owners
4. Preserve all authentication logs before making any changes
5. Check whether any login attempts succeeded — this is the critical question

Short-term response actions:
1. If any login succeeded, escalate immediately to full compromise investigation
2. Force password reset on all targeted accounts
3. Enable multi-factor authentication on targeted accounts
4. Review all activity performed by the targeted accounts in the past 30 days
5. Add source IPs to your threat intelligence blocklist
6. Check AbuseIPDB and VirusTotal for reputation of attacking IP

Long-term hardening:
1. Implement account lockout policy (lock after 5 failures, unlock after 30 minutes)
2. Deploy CAPTCHA on all public-facing login pages
3. Implement rate limiting on all authentication endpoints
4. Enforce MFA organization-wide
5. Consider geographic IP restrictions if your users are in known locations
6. Deploy a honeypot account (a username that no real user ever logs into) — any login attempt is an alert

## Section: Detection and Analysis — Port Scanning and Reconnaissance

Port scanning is automated probing of a network to discover open services. It is typically a precursor to a targeted attack.

Indicators:
- Sequential connection attempts to many different ports from a single source IP
- SYN packets sent to many ports without completing the TCP handshake
- ICMP echo requests to sequential IP addresses (ping sweep)
- Connection durations under 100 milliseconds across many ports

Risk assessment:
- Port scanning alone is low severity — it is reconnaissance, not an attack
- However, port scans almost always precede targeted attacks within 24-72 hours
- High risk if the scan reveals sensitive services: SSH (22), RDP (3389), SMB (445), database ports (1433, 3306, 5432, 27017, 6379)
- The attacker now knows your attack surface — you must assume they will use this information

Threshold rules:
- More than 20 different destination ports from same IP in 1 minute: port scan
- More than 100 different ports in 5 minutes: aggressive automated scan (Nmap, Masscan)
- Sequential port numbers: automated scanner

Immediate response:
1. Block the scanning IP at your perimeter firewall
2. Document exactly which ports were probed — the attacker knows your exposure
3. Immediately audit all open ports discovered by the scan
4. Close any unnecessary services exposed to the internet

Short-term response:
1. Conduct your own port scan against your public IP to see what attackers see
2. Close all ports that do not need to be internet-facing
3. Move sensitive services behind VPN (databases, admin panels, SSH)
4. Add the scanning IP to your watchlist — follow-up attack is likely within 72 hours
5. Check AbuseIPDB — most aggressive scanners are already known

Long-term hardening:
1. Implement a default-deny firewall policy — only explicitly permitted ports are open
2. Deploy a network intrusion detection system (Suricata or Snort) with port scan signatures
3. Consider deploying honeypot ports to detect future reconnaissance
4. Conduct regular external attack surface reviews

## Section: Detection and Analysis — Malware Incidents

Malware includes viruses, worms, Trojans, ransomware, spyware, and rootkits. Any unauthorized code that performs malicious actions.

Indicators:
- Antivirus or EDR product generates alert
- File hash matches known malicious hash in VirusTotal
- Unexpected processes running, especially those making network connections
- Encrypted files with ransom note present (ransomware)
- Unexplained high CPU, memory, or network usage
- New scheduled tasks or startup entries without authorization
- Outbound connections to unusual or unknown IP addresses

Immediate response — CRITICAL sequence:
1. ISOLATE the infected system immediately — unplug network cable or disable Wi-Fi
2. Do NOT power off the system — volatile memory contains evidence that will be destroyed
3. Do NOT attempt to run cleanup tools on the live infected system
4. Take photographs of the screen showing indicators of infection
5. Alert IT management and security team immediately
6. Do NOT pay any ransom without consulting security and legal teams

Containment:
1. Identify patient zero — which system was infected first
2. Map all systems that communicated with the infected system in the past 24-72 hours
3. Block all malware-associated IP addresses and domains at the perimeter firewall
4. Disable accounts that were active on the infected system
5. Check all systems that had contact with infected machine for signs of lateral movement

Eradication:
1. Wipe the infected system — do not attempt to clean, assume total compromise
2. Reimage from known-good baseline image
3. Verify backup integrity before restoring — some malware infects backups
4. Patch the vulnerability that allowed initial infection before bringing system back online

Recovery:
1. Restore from last known clean backup that predates the infection
2. Change all credentials that were present on the infected system
3. Monitor the restored system closely for 30 days for signs of reinfection

## Section: Detection and Analysis — Unauthorized Access

Unauthorized access is any access to systems, data, or networks by a person without permission.

Indicators:
- Login from unusual IP address, country, or at unusual time
- Access to files or directories outside the user's normal scope
- New user accounts created without authorization
- Privilege escalation events — user gaining admin rights they should not have
- Security tools disabled or logs deleted (attacker covering tracks)
- Persistence mechanisms installed: new scheduled tasks, startup scripts, registry keys

Immediate response:
1. Disable the compromised account immediately — do not just change password
2. Revoke all active sessions, tokens, and API keys for that account
3. Preserve all logs — do not alter, delete, or rotate anything
4. Determine scope: what systems, data, and resources did the attacker access

Investigation steps:
1. Pull complete login history for the compromised account — how far back does suspicious activity go
2. Enumerate all files accessed, downloaded, modified, or deleted
3. Search for persistence mechanisms: new accounts, scheduled tasks, cron jobs, modified startup scripts
4. Review all outbound network connections from affected systems — look for data exfiltration
5. Identify the initial access vector — phishing, credential theft, vulnerability exploitation

Notification and legal:
1. If personal data was accessed: GDPR or local data protection law may require regulatory notification
2. If financial data was accessed: notify relevant financial regulatory authority
3. Engage legal team within the first four hours — evidence preservation requirements apply
4. Document everything in a timeline with precise timestamps

Long-term hardening:
1. Implement multi-factor authentication on all accounts — single factor is not sufficient
2. Apply least privilege principle — users should only have access to what they need for their role
3. Deploy user behavior analytics to detect future anomalies
4. Implement privileged access management for admin accounts
5. Conduct quarterly access reviews — remove access that is no longer needed

## Section: Detection and Analysis — Data Exfiltration

Data exfiltration is unauthorized transfer of data from a system to an external destination.

Indicators:
- Unusual large outbound data transfers significantly above baseline
- Outbound connections to previously unseen external IP addresses
- Data being compressed or archived before transfer
- Connections to unauthorized cloud storage services (personal Dropbox, Google Drive, Mega)
- DNS queries for unusually long domain names (possible DNS tunneling)
- Network traffic on non-standard ports, especially outbound

Immediate response:
1. Block outbound connections to suspected exfiltration destinations at firewall immediately
2. Preserve network flow logs, firewall logs, and proxy logs before any changes
3. If insider threat is suspected: do NOT alert the suspect — preserve the investigation
4. Isolate affected systems from the network
5. Engage legal team in first hour — data breach has legal and regulatory implications

Scope assessment:
1. Identify exactly what data was exfiltrated: type, sensitivity, volume
2. Identify all destination IPs, domains, and cloud services used
3. Determine the time window — when did exfiltration begin and end
4. Determine whether the exfiltration is ongoing or completed

Legal and compliance obligations:
1. GDPR Article 33: notification to supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of breach
2. GDPR Article 34: notification to affected individuals if high risk to their rights
3. Preserve all evidence in forensically sound manner — chain of custody applies
4. Do not destroy any evidence — legal hold applies from the moment you suspect a breach

## Section: Containment, Eradication, and Recovery

Containment strategy must be selected based on:
- Time required to implement the strategy
- Effectiveness in preventing further damage
- Preservation of evidence
- Service availability requirements during containment
- Resources required

Containment options by severity:
- Low severity: monitor and allow to continue while gathering evidence
- Medium severity: isolate the affected system while keeping the rest operational
- High severity: full network isolation, take system offline

Evidence preservation rules:
- Always capture volatile data first: running processes, network connections, logged-in users, clipboard contents
- Then capture non-volatile data: file system, registry, logs
- Never work on original evidence — always work on verified forensic copies
- Document every action taken with timestamp and personnel identity

## Section: Post-Incident Activity

Every incident must be followed by a lessons learned review.

Lessons learned meeting:
- Hold within two weeks of resolving the incident
- Include all stakeholders: IR team, affected system owners, management
- Focus on process improvement, not blame assignment

Questions to answer in the review:
1. What exactly happened, and when (full timeline)?
2. How was the incident detected — was detection timely or delayed?
3. Were documented procedures followed — if not, why?
4. What information was needed that was not available?
5. What would have prevented this incident?
6. What changes to procedures, controls, or technology would prevent recurrence?
7. What precursor indicators existed that were missed?

Incident metrics to track:
- Number of incidents per category per month
- Time from occurrence to detection
- Time from detection to containment
- Time from containment to recovery
- Total cost of each incident (labor, lost productivity, remediation)

Incident report contents:
- Incident name and identifier
- Dates and times of each phase
- Personnel involved
- Steps taken and their outcomes
- Evidence collected
- Impact assessment
- Root cause
- Recommendations

## Section: DDoS Attack Response

A Distributed Denial of Service attack floods a target with traffic to exhaust resources and make services unavailable.

Indicators:
- Sudden spike in inbound traffic volume
- Service response times degrading or service becoming unavailable
- High bandwidth consumption without corresponding legitimate user activity
- Massive increase in requests from many different source IPs
- Specific ports or protocols being overwhelmed

Immediate response:
1. Confirm it is a DDoS and not a legitimate traffic spike or infrastructure failure
2. Contact your ISP or hosting provider immediately — they have upstream filtering capacity you do not
3. Activate any existing DDoS protection service (Cloudflare, AWS Shield, Akamai)
4. Enable rate limiting at load balancer and firewall level
5. Enable geo-blocking if attack is concentrated from specific regions

Mitigation:
1. Identify attack type: volumetric (floods bandwidth), protocol (exhausts connection tables), application layer (HTTP floods)
2. Apply appropriate mitigation per attack type
3. Use BGP blackholing for volumetric attacks if available through ISP
4. Use CAPTCHA challenges for application layer attacks
5. Increase server capacity temporarily if financially viable

Business continuity:
1. Communicate status to stakeholders via status page immediately
2. Prioritize protecting revenue-generating services over non-critical services
3. Maintain communication with customers even if service is degraded

Post-attack:
1. Analyze attack traffic patterns for future signature-based blocking
2. Implement permanent DDoS protection — do not wait for the next attack
3. Update incident response plan with specific playbook for DDoS
